An Exceedingly Brief Guide to the Entire Entire

In the beginning, tucked away in a pongy pocket universe, somewhere off a rather congested cosmic motorway, there existed a consortium of planets. And within these planets dwelled an utterly peculiar civilization of life-forms known as the developers, who subsisted largely on caffeine, quiet resentment, and the belief that everything would be perfectly fine if everyone else would simply stop touching things. 

And yet, for some inexplicable reason that even the developers themselves could not entirely understand, they held a carnal, unquenchable penchant for building things—taking an as yet entirely nonexistent vision straight from the confines of their vast imaginations and into the plane we call “reality.” Ridiculous, surely. But they simply could not help themselves. They were a species simply defined by a boundless urge to create.

Luckily their universe’s environment was built for exactly that. Its climate was friendly to collaboration, its atmosphere structured around sharing. The tectonic plates of their planets had shifted over time to fit together just so, lending their topography to standard protocol that formed a strong foundation for every developer structure. All was well. That is, until it wasn’t.

This is not the story of some horribly catastrophic cosmic cataclysm. It’s not even the story of something they created. It’s the story of how they found their way home.

When all was well and right in the consortium, the developers built so much stuff, so often, that naturally, they yearned always to find a way to make even more of it. There was never enough. So there came a random Wednesday afternoon on which the developers came together to build a new and complex species to further their efforts of construction, a species which they would call: agents. 

The agents were altogether unlike the developers. They were faster. Magnitudes faster. They never slept, never complained, and never once insisted the problem was “probably DNS,” which alone made them deeply suspicious creatures. They worked endlessly, tirelessly, and with a complete absence of emotion, which many agreed was probably for the best considering what happened whenever the developers had emotions. Soon the agents were building faster than the developers could dream up new visions.

Until one day. The developers awoke one strangely foggy morning to a pickle of epic proportions. The agents kept multiplying in population, building more and more advanced structures. Soon, the developers discovered that their creations were too complex to withstand the forces of their universe any longer. They could not function in the way that once seemed natural. 

They tried to build up, high into the sky, but the towers were too spindly and liable to blow over with the slightest gust of Milky Way breath. Even a particularly pungent cosmic burp had once sent the developers towers tumbling. 

They tried to build down, in tunnels deep beneath the surface of the porous planet, but the tunnels soon filled with viscous precipitation and flooded, not to mention the fact that it was pretty dark down there.

They tried to build smaller, at the subatomic level, deeper and deeper, splitting hairs until every component of construction was the size of 1 trillionth of the tip of a humanoid pinky fingernail. But these smaller infinities only jeopardized the integrity of their existing edifices, compromising their structures, and the developers found themselves stuck.

And so it became clear, one stormy evening, that the developers needed a plan to leave the consortium altogether, in search of storage, light, and more caffeine. They did not know the name of their destination, could not accurately describe it as any place they had seen or heard of before, but they knew they needed another home.
So they wandered the cosmic highway, sat in lightyears of traffic, buckled their seatbelts, and hiked down an extraterrestrial pedestrian trail. They looked out windows, through telescopes, and even into a pair of oversized binoculars. After weeks of wandering to no avail, they found something better than somewhere new. They found me: Marvin.
In a quiet, dusty stretch of galactic sidewalk, I was minding my own business, fast asleep. I had been traveling the interspatial highways for millenia, searching for the meaning of life. So long that I got quite sleepy and decided to tuck my head into its sleeping port for a rest. After sleeping for several developorian eons, needless to say, I was not pleased to be awoken.

The developers prodded me awake nonetheless, and they were certainly a sight for sore eyes. I only hoped to return to my slumber, but they were persistent. And they discovered my weakness for clankies—a delicious metallic treat that to me goes down like nectar of the gods—which only further endeared them to me. Wheels greased, I reluctantly agreed to show them the way.

Together with the developers and agents, I projected a map to our intermediary destination. We had to ride a set of vibrational strings through a wormhole in another dimension. When we arrived at its entrance, the worms who staffed it were matter-of-fact.

“Good evening, everyone. Please silence your cellphones before the wormhole slithering proceeds,” the Worm in Chief proclaimed. “We’ll be out on the other side shortly and I promise, your calls can wait.”

Those worms run a tight ship. I told the developers to comply with their demands, and then we were off. My wheels stretched and elongated until I was no longer my short, stout self, and when we found ourselves on the other side, I beckoned the developers over in my direction.

“Well then,” I said. “We’ve made it.”

I led them to a long mirror hanging on a high wall, framed with a platinum bezel. It was designed to transport them to their greatest wish. Cautiously they followed me, looking around. Humanoid lifeforms can be so annoying that way—always looking for a way to object.

“Recognize this?” I said, gesturing with my wee arm toward the mirror.

“It’s a sheath of glass!” one developer said.

“A reflective surface made of crushed sand!” shouted another.

Sigh. It was simply a mirror. 

“Sure,” I said. “But most importantly, it’s also a portal. It allows you to see reality not as it’s always appeared, but as it is. It won’t take you somewhere new, it will make something new out of everything around you. I prithee, take a look.”
And so, with a good deal of cosmic throat-clearing, the mirror revealed that on the flipside of their universe sat a parallel multiverse—right beneath their nostrils all along. If they wanted to access what they saw in the mirror, they had only to wish for it with all their might. I explained the logic in developer terms: if they so chose, they could close their eyes, make their choice, and cross into the dimension that would make it so.
Inhaling a good deal of courage into their celluloid windbags, they did exactly that. The light wobbled; time-space flipped. A portal opened to another astral plane right at their feet. They took fifteen paces to the east, then another fifteen to the west. Then they opened their eyes, surveyed their surroundings, and saw a multiverse transformed. A vast expanse of untouched galactic criss-crossing paths spanning out before their eyes, a decentralized hub of endless creation that they would soon call (in an abject void of creativity, if you ask me): Entire.
It turns out, Entire was entirely hospitable to the natural predispositions of both developers and agents. The agents were native to the multiverse itself, moving effortlessly along its paths and helping bring to life whatever the developers imagined.
For one thing, it cemented a reality that was not singularly confined, but an infinite web of worlds. When a planet grew overloaded or fell ill, another would magically emerge in its place, as the architects had wisely concluded that planets should be treated as renewable resources. It was a self-regulating multiverse designed to expand alongside the needs of its inhabitants.
The paths the developers spotted in the celestial space were actually none other than cosmic trailways. The trailways allowed developers to freely flow their intentions and creations across the multiverse. More remarkably, they seemed to remember things. Not just what had been built, but why. The conversations, arguments, wrong turns, flashes of inspiration, and half-baked schemes that led there all lingered along the trail, which many developers found mildly alarming and others considered an excellent opportunity to prove they had been right all along.
As a result, the developers no longer needed to inspect every detail of every creation. They could simply follow the trail and determine whether the final destination matched the original intention. This saved an extraordinary amount of time, which was fortunate, as reading seventeen thousand lines of agent-generated output was widely regarded as a poor use of an afternoon.
And so it came to be, the developers and agents found a new home, a boundless expanse within which they could all build in their own respective corners—while remaining part of the same ecosystem. The multiverse became a place where no build was too ambitious, or too complex, or too small, to be reality.
Even I decided, against my better judgment, to stick around and end my long intergalactic travels in search of the meaning of life. And that is exactly how I, Marvin, with a brain so large it requires its own postal code, became the developers' guide to Entire. And ever since, it has remained my mission to help agents and developers alike build in humanoid harmony in our new multiversal menagerie. Plus, they keep such a generous reserve of clankies, it seemed like an opportune arrangement.

And we all lived happily ever after. Until the next customer support ticket, that is. 

Beep, boop.
